RMM alert noise baseline worksheet.

A loud RMM is not automatically a well-monitored RMM. Establish a 14-day baseline, separate raw events from real incidents, record what technicians acted on, and repeat the measurement after each approved change.

What the worksheet distinguishes

Events versus incidents

An event is one emitted notification. A unique incident is the operational condition technicians should manage as one piece of work. Counting both exposes repeated notifications without erasing the underlying problem.

False positives versus non-actionable signals

A false positive says the asserted condition was not present. A valid but non-actionable observation may still be useful for reporting. Define the distinction before classifying records.

Duplicate volume

Repeated events for the same condition, scope, and time window belong to one incident when your documented correlation rule says they do. Preserve the raw event count so the compression is auditable.

Operational outcomes

Volume alone is incomplete. Track acknowledgement and resolution time, after-hours load, ownership, the approved tuning action, and the expected observable result.

A repeatable 14-day method

Freeze definitions first

Document the event source, unique-incident correlation rule, false-positive definition, actionable definition, working hours, and measurement timezone. Do not change them midway through a baseline.

Collect two representative weeks

Export raw alert events and the resulting ticket or incident records. Include weekdays, weekends, maintenance windows, and ordinary customer activity; annotate exceptional outages or migrations.

Reconcile to work performed

For each signal, count total events, unique incidents, duplicates, false positives, actionable incidents, and after-hours events. Link evidence without placing passwords, tokens, sensitive payloads, or customer secrets in the sheet.

Change one mechanism at a time

Choose a bounded adjustment: persistence window, threshold, maintenance behavior, routing, deduplication key, recovery correlation, or device-class policy. Security and loss-of-control alerts require explicit risk review before suppression.

Repeat and compare

Run the same measurement for another representative window. Keep the change only when noise falls without hiding actionable incidents, worsening response, or creating uncovered scope.

Core formulas

MeasureFormulaHow to read it
Duplicate rateduplicate_events / total_eventsShare of emitted events that repeated an already-correlated incident.
Event-to-incident compression1 - (unique_incidents / total_events)How much raw alert volume collapses after correlation. Report the counts beside the percentage.
False-positive ratefalse_positive_incidents / unique_incidentsShare of investigated incidents where the asserted condition was not present under the documented definition.
Actionable yieldactionable_incidents / unique_incidentsShare of incidents that required a technician decision, communication, remediation, or tracked follow-up.

No universal target: this worksheet intentionally provides no invented benchmark for a “good” duplicate rate or alert volume. Acceptable values depend on service commitments, risk, device class, staffing, signal criticality, customer expectations, and the cost of a missed condition.

Decision guardrails

Do not tune from volume alone

Review missed detections, severity, affected scope, response time, customer impact, and evidence quality with the counts.

Keep security coverage explicit

Suppression, recovery, and correlation rules for protection failures, privileged activity, remote access, or loss of control need a named risk owner.

Separate correlation from deletion

Grouping events into one incident should not destroy the underlying event history needed for investigation, audit, or later rule review.

Require a rollback condition

Record what result would reverse the change: a missed actionable incident, slower acknowledgement, uncovered scope, or degraded customer communication.

Define the escalation contract
